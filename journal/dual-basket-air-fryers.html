Every single-basket air fryer owner knows the routine by the second week. Wings first, then the fries — and by the time the second basket finishes, the first is room temperature. You either cook in shifts and eat in shifts, or you watch one dish go cold while the other crisps. It is a small problem that happens every night, which makes it a big problem.

Dual-basket air fryers are the industry's answer, and the version we have on the bench — the CHEFMAN 6-quart dual-basket oven the first supplied link resolves to — is a clean example of the pattern. Its manufacturer page lists 6 quarts (5.67 L) of total capacity split into two independent 3-quart baskets, 1700 watts, and a 200–450 °F range. The Amazon listing adds the detail that matters: the baskets are genuinely separate zones, not one cavity with a divider.

What two zones actually buy you

The honest version is narrower than the ads suggest, but it's real. Two independent baskets mean two temperatures at the same time: salmon at 390 °F on one side while broccoli gets 350 °F on the other. They also mean different finishes — crisp here, gentle there — without moving food between machines. What they don't mean is a bigger single dish: a whole chicken still won't split into two 3-quart baskets, and that is the main thing you give up versus one large cavity.

Then there's the feature the listing markets as Sync Finish. Both baskets run their own programs, and the unit staggers them so both complete together — fries that need six minutes at the end simply start six minutes later. The listing also names Hi-Fry (a burst of higher heat for crisping) and an auto shutoff, and Chefman's page documents the same controls. None of this is exotic; it is one appliance doing the scheduling you used to do in your head.

Three checks before one earns counter space

1. The footprint test. The spec sheet reads 13.38 × 11.81 × 10.63 inches and 13.3 pounds — compact enough for one counter section, heavy enough that you'll leave it there. Measure the stretch of counter you're surrendering before you fall for the feature list; that's the real price of any countertop appliance.

2. The basket-split test. Two 3-quart baskets are two side dishes, or a main plus a side for two or three people. If your usual dinner is one big roast or a family tray of lasagna, a dual-basket design doesn't help; an oven-style unit or a single large basket fits that job better. Match the shape to the meals you actually cook.

3. The heat-ceiling test. The published range tops out at 450 °F with the Hi-Fry mode, which is the difference between pale fries and browned ones. Plenty of budget baskets stop at 400 °F — fine for most cooking, but you'll notice it on the nights you want char.

Who should skip this shape entirely

One-dish households, anyone who mainly reheats leftovers, and people whose counter space is already spoken for. A basic single basket does the reheat-and-crisp job at a smaller footprint, and no amount of sync logic fixes a counter that fits nothing. If your household runs two real dishes a night, though, the math changes: two zones plus automatic scheduling is the first design that actually ends the cooking-in-shifts routine.
