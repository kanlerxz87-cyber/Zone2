Basket fryer or air-fryer oven? Choose by geometry

Marketing puts every hot-air appliance in one bucket called "air fryer." The shelf knows better. A basket fryer and an air-fryer toaster oven are different machines that happen to share a heating method, and choosing between them is mostly geometry: how much cavity you need, how much counter you can surrender, and what the top of the machine's heat range is. Here is the way we'd choose, argued only from the numbers the manufacturers publish — including the ones they disagree about.

Cavity: what the food actually sits in

A basket is a small sealed cavity around the food. Air hits harder, preheats faster, and anything loose gets tossed halfway through for even crisping — that is why basket fries beat oven fries most weeknights. An oven-style cavity trades that intensity for volume and versatility: racks, a rotisserie, room for a tray rather than a heap.

The numbers make the split concrete. The dual-basket unit on our bench holds 6 quarts total, but divided into two 3-quart zones. The single-basket TurboFry holds 8 quarts in one piece — the biggest single dish of the three. The French-door oven claims 26 quarts (24.6 L), which its manufacturer page sizes for four to six people, with a rotisserie spit and a bake pan in the box. One of these machines reheats Tuesday's leftovers; another roasts a chicken on a spit. Same category sticker, different lives.

Footprint: the tax you pay every day

Capacity you notice once; footprint you notice every day. The dual-basket unit measures 13.38 × 11.81 × 10.63 inches. The oven is a different animal at 16.9 inches deep by 15.8 wide by 14.8 tall — its doors need clearance to swing, which costs another invisible few inches of counter. Under-cabinet clearance matters too: 14.8 inches of oven height fits awkwardly under short shelves. If the machine cannot live on the counter permanently, an oven-style unit becomes a cupboard appliance, and cupboard appliances get used roughly never.

Heat range: the floor matters more than the ceiling

Everyone compares maximum temperatures, but the published floors tell you what the machine can quietly do. The basket units run 200–450 °F (dual) and 200–400 °F (TurboFry, with a Hi-Fry finishing burst marketed on its listing). The oven publishes 75–500 °F — the low floor is proof-of-work for proving dough and dehydrating, jobs a 200 °F floor can't do. If you want one machine that also toasts at 7 a.m., bakes at noon and air-fries at seven, the range — not the peak — is the tell.

An honest detour: where the sources disagree

The oven's own pages can't agree how to count its presets: the listing title says 24, the manufacturer's spec tile says 25, and its feature block says "10-in-1" while naming ten functions. The TurboFry's listing bullet says 1000 watts; its product-information block and Chefman's page both say 1700 watts. We publish both readings on the spec table and pick neither — because when two official sources conflict, "we checked and here is the conflict" is more useful to you than a confident number. Where the discrepancy changes a purchase decision (wattage does), verify against the manufacturer before spending money.

The short version

Tight counter, one or two people, mostly weeknight sides and reheats: a basket, probably dual-zone if you cook two dishes a night. Replacing a toaster and dreaming about rotisserie: the oven shape, footprint and all. One big dish for a family: the large single basket sits between those worlds — 8 quarts, one cavity, four touch presets. None of these calls require trusting anyone's adjectives; the geometry alone sorts it.
