How a page like this earns a reader

Editorial hypothesis — how a page like this earns a reader, not a measured result

  1. Answer one specific question in the title and keep the promise in the first screen. A page about what 6 Qt actually holds beats a page called 'Top Air Fryers' for a reader who already knows what they are asking.
  2. Lead with numbers the manufacturer printed. Specificity is the only thing a reader can check here, so it is also the only thing worth building a headline on.
  3. Compare rather than describe. Someone choosing between two baskets and one big basket is closer to a decision than someone browsing a list, and a decision is the moment a link gets clicked.
  4. Put the boring parts in the same page: what the cavity measures, whether it fits the counter, what happens when two official pages disagree. That is what an article can do that a listing page will not.
  5. Keep internal links deliberate — guide to article, article to guide — so a reader who finishes one page has an obvious next question to ask.
  6. Disclose plainly and mark paid links, which Google's own guidance asks for on sponsored links and the Associates agreement requires regardless.

Before any of these is published

Before any of these becomes a page, the same five checks apply that the 2026-11-15 pass wrote, kept because they are the right gates:

  1. Propose it on the improvement board with its options and its cost, so the owner chooses a path before anything is built.
  2. Do not let the article imply that the observed destination is the product the brief named. Identity is unconfirmed (F-03), and every page carries that sentence.
  3. Build every specification row from a manufacturer or Amazon page. Leave blanks blank; never infer a value to fill a column.
  4. Mark each marketplace link paid, and keep the disclosure inside the same container as the link.
  5. Date-stamp the read and re-read the page before republication, so a silent change on the source cannot pass as our mistake.
